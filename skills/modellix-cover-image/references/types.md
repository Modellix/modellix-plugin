# Cover types

Use these names. A type is the layout job, not a private catalog code.

| Type | What the frame does | Use it when |
| --- | --- | --- |
| hero | One large focal object fills most of the frame. The title sits on it. | A launch, a release, or an announcement that needs a single dramatic object. |
| conceptual | Abstract shapes stand for the idea, in separate clean zones. | Architecture, a system, an API, or a method. |
| typography | The title is the picture. Supporting shapes stay small. | An opinion, a quotation, or a line that can stand alone. |
| metaphor | One concrete object carries an abstract idea. | Growth, reflection, or a meaning the article does not draw literally. |
| scene | A place and a light set the story. | A journey, a place, or a lived experience. |
| minimal | One shape, and more empty ground than object. | A single essential idea that should feel quiet. |

## Composition

- hero: the focal object takes about two thirds of the frame. The title overlays a calm area of that object.
- conceptual: two or three zones. Each zone is one idea. Do not turn the zones into a labeled diagram.
- typography: the title takes about half the frame. One small supporting shape is enough.
- metaphor: one object, readable at a glance, with a few symbolic parts. Do not add a second metaphor.
- scene: environment, light, and one narrative detail. The title stays in a quiet band.
- minimal: one shape and about two thirds empty ground.

Whitespace is part of the type. A hero may be denser than a minimal cover. Neither type fills the frame edge to edge with detail.

Illustrated people are simplified figures in the confirmed rendering: a silhouette, a flat shape, or a few marks for hair and clothes. Do not ask for a photographic face unless the user supplied a portrait reference and asked to keep that person. Even then, match the confirmed rendering rather than switching the cover into a photograph.
